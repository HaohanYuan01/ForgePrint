## A model label can be forged

Classifiers can often identify which LLM produced an unmodified response: ours reach **85.9% accuracy**. But a high-confidence model label is **not proof** of who or what originally generated the text.

ForgePrint rewrites an LLM response to preserve its meaning while making it look like it came from a **chosen target model**. Across four domains, it achieves a **60.8% attack success rate**. On CNN/DailyMail, it reaches **70.2%**.

In short: **text can be made to look like it was written by one model even when it was originally generated by another.**

## Evading a source is not reaching a target

Attacks on authorship and AI-text detectors pursue **source evasion**: any label but the original counts as success. Targeted transfer is harder &mdash; the prediction has to land on the *one* model you chose.

The two measures disagree, and evasion is the flattering one. Planner TST and ForgePrint-4B differ by **10.5 points of source evasion** but **27.7 points of targeted success** (33.1% vs. 60.8%).

## How ForgePrint works

Targeted rewrite supervision does not exist, so ForgePrint builds it offline and then distils it. For each source&rarr;target path, a frozen bank of rewrite operators is kept only where it raises a surrogate's target margin; a Teacher combines those operators with retrieved examples to generate and rank candidates; a 4B Student learns the selected rewrites through SFT, DPO, and GRPO ranked **by factuality first and target margin second**. At deployment the Student sees only the source summary and the two model names &mdash; one forward pass, no operator bank, no surrogate, no access to the original document.

## Results

The attacker's surrogate is trained on Corpus A; the four held-out evaluators (RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2, TF-IDF) are trained only on Corpus B and are **never queried by the attack**. The two corpora share no documents.

## Which target you aim at matters more than where you start

Claude is the hardest target at 39.7% mean ASR against 64.1&ndash;70.3% for the others, and that difficulty swings by domain &mdash; transfer to Claude ranges from **5% on ArXiv to 70% on CNN/DM**. Across the 48 path-by-domain cells, the target explains **82.6%** of the path effect, leaving 17.4% to the source.

## From an open model to a chosen commercial one

Gemma-4-26B-A4B writes the original CNN/DM summaries and the rewrite is aimed at one named commercial model. Every path starts near zero &mdash; an unmodified Gemma summary is almost never mistaken for Claude (0.1%) or Grok (0.5%) &mdash; and ends between **49.6% and 81.5%**.

## Takeaways

- **A classifier can be right most of the time and still be wrong about where a particular text began.** Our held-out classifiers reach 85.9% accuracy on unchanged summaries, but ForgePrint achieves a **70.2% attack success rate** on CNN/DailyMail while preserving their meaning.
- **Simply making a source label disappear is not the same as choosing a new one.** Looking only at evasion hides the gap: a 10.5-point difference in evasion becomes a **27.7-point difference** when the goal is to redirect attribution to a specific model.
- **This exposes a real-world substitution threat:** a provider could serve a small open-source model while making its outputs look like they came from a commercial API. Using a 4B rewriter, we move open-model outputs toward a chosen commercial-model label with a **68.3% attack success rate**.

## Ethics and release

Targeted rewriting is dual-use, so we keep the experiments contained: everything runs offline on generated summaries and offline classifiers, we query commercial APIs only to produce summaries, and we never test impersonation against a deployed service.

Our code and evaluation suite will be released at [github.com/HaohanYuan01/ForgePrint](https://github.com/HaohanYuan01/ForgePrint). The trained rewriter is available on request under the terms of the Ethics Statement.
